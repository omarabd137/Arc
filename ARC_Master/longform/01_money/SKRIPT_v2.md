# ARC Longform #01 – HOW MONEY REALLY WORKS (v2)
**Titel (Test folgt):** How Money Really Works – What Banks And The Rich Don't Tell You
**Länge:** ca. 15–16 Min. (2.172 Sprechwörter bei 140–150 Wörtern/Min. plus Bildpausen)
**Erzähler:** Jason (ruhig, seriös, spannend)
**Story:**
- **Jake und Ryan**, zwei Freunde aus derselben Straße: gleiche Schule, gleicher Job, gleiches Gehalt.
- Wir folgen ihnen 20 Jahre lang, und an ihrem Leben decken wir **8 Geheimnisse des Geldes** auf. Jake macht alles so, wie man es ihm beigebracht hat. Ryan lernt, wie das Spiel wirklich funktioniert.
- Dazu als roter Faden **der Dollarschein**, der am Anfang fällt und am Ende auf dem Tisch liegt.

**Regeln:** Jedes Geheimnis endet mit einer offenen Frage, die ins nächste zieht. Zahlen immer groß im Bild. Jake und Ryan sind wiedererkennbar (Jake: Kapuzenpulli, Rucksack; Ryan: Hemd, Uhr). Kein Schulton.

---

## 0:00 – COLD OPEN
[VISUAL: Pitch black. A single dollar bill drifts down into a beam of light.]

This is a dollar bill.
It costs about four cents to make.

And almost everything you were taught about it… is wrong.

[VISUAL: A quiet American street. Two houses side by side. Two kids on bikes.]

This is Jake. And this is Ryan.
They grew up on the same street. Same school. Same grades.
At 25, they even start at the same company. Same job. Same salary.

[VISUAL: Two identical paychecks: $60,000 a year.]

Twenty years later…

[VISUAL: Jake at 45, in a small rented apartment, staring at a credit card bill. Ryan at 45, on the porch of a house he owns, checking his investments.]

…one of them is a millionaire.
The other one is still renting, and still in debt.

Ryan isn't smarter. He didn't win the lottery. He didn't get lucky.

He just learned something Jake never did:
how money really works.
What the banks know. What the rich know.

And in the next 15 minutes, you're going to learn it too.

[TITLE CARD: HOW MONEY REALLY WORKS]

---

## 1:45 – SECRET #1: YOUR MONEY ISN'T IN THE BANK
[VISUAL: Jake, 25, proudly deposits his first big paycheck. The banking app shows $5,000.]

It starts with Jake's first paycheck.
He puts it in the bank. Safe. Exactly where you're supposed to.

At least, that's what he thinks.

[VISUAL: The bill Jake deposits slides through the counter… then straight out the back door to someone else.]

Here's the first thing nobody tells you:
The moment you deposit money, it stops being yours… in the way you think.

Legally, you've just lent your money to the bank.
Your balance isn't a pile of cash with your name on it.
It's a promise. The bank owes you.

[VISUAL: Jake's $5,000 turns into a paper note: "IOU – The Bank".]

And the bank doesn't keep your money lying around.
It uses it. Lends it. Invests it.

[VISUAL: Jake's $5,000 travels across town: part of it becomes a car loan for a stranger, part of it a business loan, part of it government bonds.]

While Jake sleeps, his paycheck is already working, for the bank.
It might be financing a stranger's car at seven or eight percent interest.
Jake gets almost nothing for it. The bank keeps the difference.

And Jake? He doesn't even know it's gone. His app still shows $5,000.
Because the app doesn't show the money.
It shows the promise.

For decades, American banks at least had to keep a small part of their deposits in reserve.
In March 2020, that requirement was lowered to zero.
Zero percent.

[VISUAL: "0%" slams onto the screen.]

So how much money is actually sitting in the vault, waiting for Jake?

Far less than he thinks.

But that's not even the strangest part.
Because the bank doesn't just lend out Jake's money.
It can create money that never existed.

---

## 3:30 – SECRET #2: BANKS CREATE MONEY OUT OF NOTHING
[VISUAL: Ryan, 28, in a bank office. A loan officer slides a mortgage contract across the desk. A small house on the paper.]

A few years later, Ryan buys his first house.
He takes out a 300,000-dollar mortgage.

Most people imagine the bank opens its vault, takes 300,000 dollars that other customers saved, and hands it over.

That's not what happens.

[VISUAL: The loan officer types. On Ryan's account: $300,000 appears out of nowhere.]

The bank types 300,000 dollars into existence.
One second, the money doesn't exist. The next second, it does.

This isn't a conspiracy theory.
The Bank of England, one of the oldest central banks in the world, said it in plain language in 2014:
The majority of money in the modern economy is created by commercial banks… making loans.

[VISUAL: The official document. The sentence highlighted in yellow.]

And it works in reverse.
Every month, when Ryan pays back his mortgage, that money doesn't go back into a vault.
It's deleted.

[VISUAL: Ryan's monthly payments flow into the bank and dissolve into thin air.]

Think about what this means.

Almost every dollar in the system was born as somebody's debt.
If everyone paid back every debt tomorrow, most of the money in the world would simply disappear.

Money is debt.

[VISUAL: A giant debt counter climbing: $18,800,000,000,000.]

By the end of 2025, American households owed about 18.8 trillion dollars.
Mortgages. Car loans. Student loans. Credit cards.

And now look at Ryan's mortgage from the bank's side.

[VISUAL: Ryan's contract. A calculator. The numbers add up line by line.]

300,000 dollars, over 30 years, at seven percent interest.
By the time Ryan has paid it off, he'll have paid the bank about 718,000 dollars.
More than 400,000 of that is just interest.

[VISUAL: "$418,000 INTEREST" stamps across the contract.]

Let that sink in.
The bank created the 300,000 dollars out of nothing…
and will collect more than 400,000 dollars in interest for it.

And if money is debt… then someone is making a fortune from all that debt.
Want to guess who else?

---

## 5:30 – SECRET #3: HOW THE BANK MAKES MONEY ON YOU
[VISUAL: Split screen. Left: Jake's savings account. Right: Jake's credit card.]

Let's look at Jake's money from the bank's side.

Jake keeps his savings in a regular savings account.
For that, many big banks pay him a tiny interest rate. Often well under one percent.

At the same time, Jake carries a balance on his credit card.
In 2025, the average credit card interest rate in America was around 21 percent.

[VISUAL: Two numbers side by side: "under 1%" in tiny font, "21%" in giant red letters.]

So the bank borrows Jake's money for almost nothing…
and lends money back to people like Jake for twenty times more.

That gap is one of the oldest business models on Earth.

[VISUAL: Jake's card statement. A tiny line at the bottom: "Minimum payment".]

And the card companies have a favorite trick: the minimum payment.

Say Jake owes 5,000 dollars at 21 percent, and only pays the minimum each month.
It can take him around 19 years to pay it off.
And he'll pay roughly 7,700 dollars in interest. More than the original debt.

[VISUAL: Jake ages from 25 to 44 while a tiny progress bar crawls forward.]

And if you think that's bad, the price of money gets even worse the poorer you are.
A typical payday loan charges 15 dollars for every 100 dollars borrowed, for just two weeks.
According to America's consumer protection agency, that works out to an annual rate of almost 400 percent.

But there's another one most people never notice.

[VISUAL: Jake taps his card at a coffee shop. A tiny coin flies off the payment toward a skyscraper.]

Every time you pay with a card, the store pays a fee. Often around two percent.
In 2024, American businesses paid about 187 billion dollars in card fees.

And where do you think stores get that money?

[VISUAL: The price tag on Jake's coffee quietly ticks up a few cents.]

From you. It's built into the prices.
Even when you pay cash, you pay for everyone's cards.

The bank makes money when you save.
It makes money when you borrow.
It makes money when you shop.

But the biggest money machine of all isn't a bank.
It's the place where new money is born.

---

## 8:00 – SECRET #4: THE LEAK – WHO GETS NEW MONEY FIRST
[VISUAL: The Federal Reserve building in Washington at night. A giant digital faucet on its roof.]

This is the Federal Reserve, America's central bank.

When there's a crisis, the Fed can create money on a scale no normal bank ever could.
No printing press needed. It buys bonds from banks and pays with money it creates on a computer.

[VISUAL: A rising bar chart: 2007 → 2022.]

Before the 2008 financial crisis, everything the Fed owned was worth under 900 billion dollars.
By 2022, it was almost 9 trillion. Ten times more.

When the pandemic hit, it jumped in just one year from about 4.2 trillion to more than 7.5 trillion.

[VISUAL: To scale: a stack of $100 bills rising past skyscrapers.]

If you spent a million dollars every single day, it would take you more than 20,000 years to spend 8 trillion.

So where did all that money go?

Here's the part that changes how you see everything.

[VISUAL: Money pours out of the faucet and flows downhill like a river. First it reaches banks. Then big investors. Then stock charts and houses, which shoot upward. Far downstream, Jake waits with an empty bucket.]

New money doesn't arrive everywhere at once.
It enters at the top. Through banks. Through financial markets.

The people closest to it get it first, and they spend it before prices rise.
Everyone else gets it last.

Economists call this the Cantillon effect, named after an 18th-century banker who noticed it 300 years ago.

And in 2020, you could watch it happen live.

[VISUAL: Ryan's house value climbs. Ryan's investment app turns green.]

American home prices rose more than 30 percent in just two years.
The stock market doubled from its 2020 low in under 18 months.

People who owned houses and stocks… like Ryan… got richer without doing anything.

[VISUAL: Ryan's house with a price sign: $300,000 → $393,000.]

Ryan's 300,000-dollar house was suddenly worth around 390,000.
He gained about 90,000 dollars in two years, while sleeping.
More than his salary for an entire year.

[VISUAL: Jake opens a letter. His rent goes up. Again.]

People who owned nothing… like Jake… just watched everything become more expensive.

Same salary. Same street.
Completely different result.

And there's one more force quietly working against Jake, every single day.
One he never agreed to.

---

## 10:15 – SECRET #5: THE INVISIBLE TAX
[VISUAL: A grocery store in 1971. A shopping cart filled to the top. Price tag: $100.]

In 1971, 100 dollars could fill a cart like this.

Today, to buy the same things, you need about 800 dollars.

[VISUAL: The same cart today. The bill on the table shrinks.]

Nobody stole your money.
It's still the same bill.
It just buys less. Every year.

That's inflation. And here's what most people never hear:
It's not an accident.

[VISUAL: A Fed document. "2 percent" circled in red.]

The Federal Reserve officially aims for prices to rise by about two percent every year.

Two percent sounds small.
But over 35 years, two percent a year cuts what your money can buy in half.

[VISUAL: Jake's savings account: the number stays the same, but the groceries it can buy shrink to half a bag.]

So Jake, saving responsibly in his bank account at under one percent…
is actually getting poorer every year. Slowly. Quietly.

[VISUAL: Jake's $20,000 savings. Each year, a slice quietly evaporates.]

Let's say Jake has saved 20,000 dollars, earning half a percent.
If prices rise by three percent that year, his savings lose around 500 dollars in real value.
In one year. Without him spending a single cent.

Do that for ten years, and Jake has worked months of his life for money that simply melted away.

And Ryan?
His house and his stocks tend to rise with prices.

Inflation melts cash.
And it rewards people who own things.

Which brings us to the question you've been waiting for:
How do the really rich play this game?

---

## 12:00 – SECRET #6: HOW THE RICH PLAY THE GAME
[VISUAL: A glass office at the top of a skyscraper. A billionaire in a quiet suit looks over the city.]

Jake earns money, pays tax on it, and saves what's left.
Ryan earns money and buys assets.

But the richest people on Earth go one step further.

They barely use income at all.

[VISUAL: The billionaire's shares glow on a screen, growing.]

Their wealth sits in shares of their companies.
And shares that aren't sold aren't taxed.

So when they need money for a house, a jet, a new company… they don't sell.

[VISUAL: He hands the bank a stack of shares. The bank hands him a suitcase of money. The shares stay with him.]

They borrow. Using their shares as collateral.

A loan isn't income. So it isn't taxed like income.
The shares keep growing. The loans get paid with new loans.

[VISUAL: A simple scale. Left: $1 billion in shares growing. Right: a $10 million loan.]

Imagine someone with one billion dollars in shares.
If those shares grow eight percent in a year, that's 80 million dollars of new wealth.
They borrow 10 million to live on. At five percent, the interest is 500,000 dollars.

No salary. No sale. And no income tax on the 10 million.
The wealth keeps growing faster than the debt.

And when they die?

[VISUAL: A will on a desk. The shares pass to a child.]

Under American tax law, their heirs inherit those shares at today's value.
All the gains that built up over a lifetime are never taxed as capital gains.

In finance, this has a dark nickname:
Buy. Borrow. Die.

[VISUAL: Three words appear one by one in white.]

In 2021, the newsroom ProPublica got hold of secret IRS data.
They compared what the 25 richest Americans paid in taxes to how much their wealth grew.
Between 2014 and 2018, that was what ProPublica called a "true tax rate" of just 3.4 percent.

[VISUAL: "3.4%" in giant numbers next to Jake's payslip.]

It's legal.
It's just a different game.

And it all runs on one thing Jake has been taught to fear:
debt.

---

## 14:00 – SECRET #7: THE PRICE OF MONEY
[VISUAL: Split screen. Jake at a store checkout with a new TV on his credit card. The billionaire signing a loan.]

Here's the cruelest part of the whole system.

Money has a price. It's called interest.
And the price is not the same for everyone.

Jake pays around 21 percent on his credit card.
The billionaire, borrowing against millions in shares, can often borrow for a fraction of that.

[VISUAL: Two price tags for the same $1,000: Jake's is huge and red, the billionaire's is small.]

The less money you have, the more money costs you.
The more money you have, the cheaper money gets.

And look at what each one borrows for.

Jake borrows to buy things that lose value. A TV. A car. A vacation.
The rich borrow to buy things that gain value. Companies. Buildings. Shares.

For most people, debt is a trap.
For the people who understand money, debt is a tool.

Same thing. Opposite result.

But what happens when the people playing with all this debt get it wrong?

---

## 15:15 – SECRET #8: HEADS THEY WIN, TAILS YOU LOSE
[VISUAL: California, March 2023. A modern glass building: Silicon Valley Bank. Phones lighting up everywhere.]

March 9th, 2023.
Silicon Valley Bank, one of the biggest banks in America.

A rumor spreads. Group chats explode.
"Get your money out."

[VISUAL: Hundreds of phones, all tapping "Transfer" at the same second.]

In a single day, customers tried to withdraw 42 billion dollars.

But remember Secret Number One.
The money was never all there.

[VISUAL: An empty vault. The lights go out.]

The next morning, regulators shut the bank down.

In America, deposits are only insured up to 250,000 dollars. Most of the money at this bank was above that limit.
And yet, two days later, the government guaranteed every single dollar. Even above the limit.

Why? Because if trust breaks at one bank, people start running from the next.

[VISUAL: Flashback, 2008. Wall Street in chaos. Then Congress, approving a giant check.]

We've seen this before.
In 2008, when the big banks gambled and lost, Congress approved a 700-billion-dollar rescue program.

[VISUAL: March 2009. An AIG office tower. Envelopes with checks being handed out.]

And a few months later, the insurance giant AIG, which had just been rescued with taxpayer money,
paid out around 165 million dollars in bonuses to its employees.
According to New York's attorney general, 73 of them received at least one million dollars each.

[VISUAL: Jake watching it on the news, shaking his head.]

When the system wins, the profits are private.
When it loses, the losses become everyone's problem.

Heads, they win.
Tails… you lose.

---

## 16:45 – FINALE: THE DOLLAR ON THE TABLE
[VISUAL: Back to the beginning. Jake and Ryan at 45, sitting at the same kitchen table where they grew up. Between them, the single dollar bill.]

So let's go back to Jake and Ryan.

Same street. Same school. Same salary.

Jake did everything he was told.
He saved cash. He borrowed for things. He trusted the bank to keep his money.

Ryan learned the rules.
Cash loses value. So he owned things.
New money flows to assets first. So he owned assets.
Debt is a tool. So he only used it to buy things that grow.

[VISUAL: Four rules appear one by one on the table next to the dollar.]

[VISUAL: A "what if" timeline for Jake. A small coin drops into an investment jar every month.]

And here's the part that hurts the most.

If Jake had simply put 300 dollars a month into a broad stock market fund from age 25,
and it had grown at the market's long-term average of around ten percent a year, which is never guaranteed,
by 45 he would have had around 228,000 dollars.
From just 72,000 dollars of his own money.

The rest? The same force the rich use every day. Owning things that grow.

Neither of them did anything illegal.
Neither of them was smarter.

The only difference was knowing how money really works.

[VISUAL: Close-up on the dollar bill. Four cents of cotton and linen.]

Look at that dollar again.
Four cents of paper.
Most money like it was never printed. Only typed into existence, the moment someone took on debt.
And it's worth a little less every year.

It works for one reason only: we all keep believing in it.

[VISUAL: Jake picks up the bill. For the first time, he looks at it differently.]

Now you know the rules.

Most people never will.

[END CARD: ARC]
